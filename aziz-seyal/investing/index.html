How an REO purchase actually differs.

Bank-owned, short-sale, multi-family and commercial purchases — explained as general information about how these transactions work.

Bank-Owned Short Sales Multi-Family Commercial

Bank-owned (REO)

When a property has gone through foreclosure and not sold at auction, the lender takes ownership and lists it — "real estate owned." The differences from a conventional purchase are practical ones:

  • As-is condition. The lender has usually never lived in the property and typically sells without repairs or seller disclosures in the usual form.
  • The bank's addenda. Lenders attach their own contract addenda, which can override terms in a standard agreement. Each one is read and explained before you sign.
  • Timelines. Responses to offers and requests follow the lender's internal process rather than a homeowner's.
  • Financing constraints. Some loan types have property-condition requirements that a vacant or distressed property may not meet. Talk to your lender early.

Short sales

In a short sale the owner still holds title, but the lender must agree to accept less than the amount owed. That adds a separate lender-approval stage after a buyer and seller agree terms, and the approval can change or delay the deal. [CONFIRM wording]

Multi-family

Two-, three- and four-family buildings bring existing leases, separate utilities, rental-unit inspections and municipal requirements into the purchase. What those documents say — and what they don't — is reviewed before an offer. [CONFIRM]
