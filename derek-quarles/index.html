Owner-occupied multi-family

Live in one.
Rent the other.

A large share of Bridgeport's homes are two-, three- and four-family buildings. Buying one to live in while renting the other units is a common path to ownership here — and it is a different transaction from a single-family purchase. Here is how it works.

  • What counts as an owner-occupied two-to-four familyA building with two, three or four units where you live in one as your primary home. Lenders set how long you must live there and how that is documented. [CONFIRM]
  • How lenders treat a property with more than one unitLoan programmes, down-payment requirements and reserve rules can differ from a single family, and some lenders consider existing leases when reviewing an application. [CONFIRM]
  • What an appraisal looks at differentlyThe appraiser compares the building with other multi-unit sales and, for some loan types, completes a schedule of the units and their leases. [CONFIRM]
  • What you take on as a landlord from day oneExisting leases and tenants' security deposits transfer with the building at closing, along with a landlord's responsibilities under Connecticut law. [CONFIRM]
  • What to check before you make an offerCopies of current leases, how utilities are metered, the legal number of units on record with the city, and the condition of each unit's heating, plumbing and electrical. [CONFIRM]

General information only. This describes how these purchases work. It is not financial, tax, legal or lending advice, and it makes no prediction about rents, costs or returns. Every programme rule and requirement must be checked against current rules [CONFIRM].

How is buying a two-to-four family different from buying a single family?

Mostly in the details around the building. Loan programmes and their requirements can differ when a property has more than one unit, the appraisal looks at comparable multi-unit sales, the inspection covers every unit's systems, and existing leases and security deposits pass to you at closing.
