The buying path

Seven steps, and the place people lose money in each one.

Nothing here is a secret. It is just rarely laid out in order, and almost never with the mistakes attached.

Step 01

Pre-approval

A lender reviews income, debts, credit and assets and tells you what they will actually lend. It is not a pre-qualification, which is a conversation, and it is not a guarantee, which nothing is.

What people get wrong: touring first. You spend three weekends calibrating to houses you cannot buy, or worse, ones you could have beaten.
Step 02

Budget reality

What the lender approves and what you want to pay every month are two different numbers. The second one is the one that matters, and it includes taxes, insurance, any HOA, and the maintenance a first-time buyer has never had to budget for.

What people get wrong: treating the approval amount as the target. It is a ceiling, and ceilings are not goals.
Step 03

Touring

Narrow to what fits the budget and the requirements, then look at enough to calibrate. Photograph everything — by the fourth house they blur, and the notes you did not take are the ones you needed.

What people get wrong: falling for the staging. Furniture leaves. Layout, light, storage and what is behind the walls do not.
Step 04

Offer strategy

Price is one of several terms, and often not the one that wins. Timelines, deposit, contingency periods and flexibility on the close date all carry weight, and they cost you differently.

What people get wrong: competing only on price, then waiving protections to make up the difference — without pricing what the waiver just cost them.
Step 05

Inspection and contingencies

A contingency is a written condition that lets you renegotiate or withdraw if something specific does not check out. The inspection is where you find out what the house actually is, as opposed to what it looks like.

What people get wrong: reading the inspection report as a list of demands. It is information — some of it is a negotiation, some of it is a reason to walk, and most of it is simply what owning a house is like.
Step 06

Escrow

A neutral third party holds funds and documents while the appraisal, the loan, the disclosures and the title work all complete. It runs on deadlines, and missing one has consequences written into the contract.

What people get wrong: opening new credit or changing jobs mid-escrow. The loan is re-verified before it funds, and that is a genuinely awful week to discover it.
