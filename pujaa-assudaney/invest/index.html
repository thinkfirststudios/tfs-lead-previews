A rental either works on paper or it doesn’t.

Cash flow and appreciation are two different bets

People say “investment property” as if it is one thing. It is at least two, and they pull in different directions.

Cash flow is what the property produces every month once everything is paid. It is knowable, it is boring, and it either exists or it doesn’t. Appreciation is what the property might be worth later. It is unknowable, it is the part people daydream about, and it is the reason otherwise sensible buyers accept a property that loses money monthly.

A property that costs you money every month is a bet on appreciation whether or not you meant to make one. That is not automatically a bad decision — it is just a different decision from the one most people think they are making, and it should be made deliberately.

Where this goes wrong: the spreadsheet is built on rent minus mortgage. Those two lines are the easiest to find and the least complete picture available.

The lines people leave out

None of these are exotic. All of them are permanent, and all of them come out of the same monthly number.

  • VacancyNo property is rented every month of every year. A tenant leaves, the unit turns over, and the mortgage does not pause while that happens.
  • MaintenanceNot renovation — the ordinary attrition. Water heaters, HVAC, plumbing, paint, appliances. It averages out over years and arrives all at once.
  • Capital expenditureThe big-ticket replacements that are not annual but are certain: roof, HVAC system, sewer line. A roof you did not budget for is not an emergency, it is arithmetic you skipped.
  • Property managementWhether you pay a manager or do it yourself, it costs. If you do it yourself, you have taken a job, and it is worth pricing that job honestly.
  • InsuranceLandlord policies are not homeowner policies. [CONFIRM current market conditions for California landlord insurance — this line has moved significantly and quoting anything stale would be misleading]
  • Property taxIn California, reassessment on purchase is the part that surprises buyers who priced from the seller’s existing tax bill. That bill reflects the seller’s basis, not yours.
  • HOAWhere one applies, it is a fixed monthly cost with its own rules about renting at all.
  • Leasing costsAdvertising, screening, and the time between listing and a signed lease.
The honest test: put every line above into the spreadsheet before you look at the rent. If the property still works, you have something. If it only works when you leave three of them out, you have found out something useful.

What a first purchase actually involves

Step 01

Cash flow, appreciation, or a mix — and what you would need to be true for it to have been a good decision in five years. Written down, before you look at listings.

Every cost line above, filled in for this address, with real quotes rather than rules of thumb. This is the part where a deal either survives or gets abandoned cheaply.

Step 04

Inspect it like a landlord, not a buyer

You are not deciding whether you would live here. You are deciding what will break, when, and how much of the next three years’ cash flow it will consume.

Step 05

Understand the tenancy you are inheriting

An occupied property comes with a lease, a rent history, a deposit you become responsible for, and tenant protections that apply regardless of the sale. About 1031 exchanges

A 1031 exchange lets an investor defer capital gains tax by rolling proceeds from one investment property into another, under section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code. That is the concept, and it is as far as this page goes.

It is IRS-governed, it runs on strict deadlines, and it requires a qualified intermediary engaged before the sale closes — not afterwards, when it is too late. It also requires tax counsel. A real estate licensee can tell you the exchange exists and work within its timeline; deciding whether it fits your situation, and executing it correctly, is a CPA and attorney matter.
